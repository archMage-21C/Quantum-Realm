# Representation of a Wave function in Hilbert Space
## 1. What is a "Wave Function" or "State Vector"?

In classical mechanics, we describe the "state" of a particle by its position $x$ and momentum $p$. Given those, we know everything about the system.

In quantum mechanics, the "state" of the system is the **wave function**. It's the most complete possible description of the system.

The foundational idea of the formalism is this:

> **The state of any quantum system is represented by a vector in a complex vector space called a Hilbert space**.

This abstract vector is the "wave function" in its most general form. We often write it as a "ket": $|\psi\rangle$.

**Intuition:**

Let's think of a simple system with only two possible outcomes. For example, a "quantum coin" that can be heads or tails.

- We can define a basis vector for "heads": $|H\rangle$
- We can define a basis vector for "tails": $|T\rangle$

These two vectors are **orthogonal** (mutually exclusive) and **normalized** (unit length). They form the "axes" of our 2-dimensional Hilbert space.

In classical physics, the coin is either 100% $|H\rangle$ or 100% $|T\rangle$.

In quantum physics, the state of the coin (its "wave function" $|\psi\rangle$) can be any combination of these two. This is the superposition principle: $$|\psi\rangle = \alpha |H\rangle + \beta |T\rangle$$
This vector $|\psi\rangle$ _is_ the wave function. The simple list of its components $(\alpha, \beta)$ is its representation in the $\{|H\rangle, |T\rangle\}$ basis.

## 2. What Do the Amplitudes ($\alpha$, $\beta$) Represent?

The complex numbers $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in the state vector $|\psi\rangle = \alpha |H\rangle + \beta |T\rangle$ are called **probability amplitudes**.

Their physical meaning is defined by the **Born Rule**, which connects the vector to a measurable, real-world experiment:

- **$|\alpha|^2$ is the probability** of finding the system in the state $|H\rangle$ (getting "heads") if you perform a measurement.
- **$|\beta|^2$ is the probability** of finding the system in the state $|T\rangle$ (getting "tails") if you perform a measurement.

**Interpretation:**

The state $|\psi\rangle$ doesn't mean the coin is "secretly" heads or tails and we just don't know. It means the coin exists in a state of indefiniteness defined by these two amplitudes. The act of measurement "forces" the system to choose one of these two outcomes, and the probabilities of its choice are governed by the amplitudes.

Because the total probability must be 100%, we have the normalization condition:

$$|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$$

This just means the state vector $|\psi\rangle$ must be a unit vector (length 1) in the Hilbert space.

If we have an n-dimensional system (e.g., a quantum die with $n$ faces $\{|1\rangle, \dots, |n\rangle\}$), the general state is:

$$|\psi\rangle = \alpha_1 |1\rangle + \alpha_2 |2\rangle + \dots + \alpha_n |n\rangle$$

And the probability of measuring outcome $k$ is $P(k) = |\alpha_k|^2$, with the condition $\sum_k |\alpha_k|^2 = 1$.

## 3. Why Are the Amplitudes Complex Numbers?

If we only cared about _probabilities_, real numbers would be sufficient. We could have $\alpha = 0.8$ and $\beta = 0.6$, giving $P(H) = (0.8)^2 = 0.64$ and $P(T) = (0.6)^2 = 0.36$. The sum is $1.0$. This works.

The amplitudes **must be complex** to explain the "wave-like" part of quantum mechanics, i.e. **interference**.

**Intuition: The "Two-Path" Problem**

Imagine our quantum coin can get from an initial state $|S\rangle$ to a final state $|F\rangle$ by two different paths (Path 1 and Path 2).

- The amplitude for it to take Path 1 is $A_1$.
- The amplitude for it to take Path 2 is $A_2$.

The total amplitude for the process is the sum of the individual amplitudes (this is the superposition principle again): $$A_{\text{total}} = A_1 + A_2$$

The probability of the process happening is then the squared modulus of this total amplitude: $$P = |A_{\text{total}}|^2 = |A_1 + A_2|^2$$

Now, let's write the complex amplitudes $A_1$ and $A_2$ in polar form:
$A_1 = r_1 e^{i\theta_1}$, $A_2 = r_2 e^{i\theta_2}$

(where $r_1^2 = P_1$ is the probability of just going through Path 1, and $r_2^2 = P_2$ is the probability for Path 2).

The total probability is:

$$P = |r_1 e^{i\theta_1} + r_2 e^{i\theta_2}|^2$$
$$P = (r_1 e^{-i\theta_1} + r_2 e^{-i\theta_2})(r_1 e^{i\theta_1} + r_2 e^{i\theta_2})$$
$$P = r_1^2 + r_2^2 + r_1 r_2 (e^{i(\theta_1 - \theta_2)} + e^{-i(\theta_1 - \theta_2)})$$

Using Euler's formula ($e^{i\phi} + e^{-i\phi} = 2\cos(\phi)$):

$$P = P_1 + P_2 + 2\sqrt{P_1 P_2} \cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2)$$

This is the central result. The total probability is _not_ just the sum of the probabilities ($P_1 + P_2$). It has a third part: **$2\sqrt{P_1 P_2} \cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2)$** is the **interference term**.

The **phase** ($\theta_1, \theta_2$) of the complex amplitude is what "waves."

- If the phases align ($\theta_1 = \theta_2$), the $\cos(0)=1$, and the probability is _higher_ than the sum. This is **constructive interference**.
- If the phases are opposite ($\theta_1 - \theta_2 = \pi$), the $\cos(\pi)=-1$, and the probability is _lower_ than the sum. This is **destructive interference**.

**Conclusion:** We need complex numbers because:

1. Their **magnitudes-squared** give us **probabilities**.
2. Their **phases** carry the information about **interference**, which is the defining "wave" behavior of quantum mechanics.

A state like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|H\rangle + |T\rangle)$ is physically different from a state like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|H\rangle + i|T\rangle)$. They both give a 50/50 chance of being measured as $H$ or $T$, but their _relative phase_ is different, and this difference is physically real and measurable (e.g., in a different basis).

## 4. What Are the Basis Vectors?

The basis vectors are the "coordinate axes" of our Hilbert space. Their physical interpretation is one of the most important concepts in the formalism:

> **Each basis vector represents a specific, definite, and mutually exclusive _outcome_ of a physical measurement.**

The choice of _which_ basis to use is equivalent to choosing _what_ we want to measure.

### Examples:
1. **Discrete Basis (e.g., a Qubit/Spin-1/2):**
    - **The Measurement:** You decide to measure the electron's spin along the $z$-axis.
    - **The Outcomes:** There are only two possible, definite results you can get: "spin up" or "spin down."
    - **The Basis Vectors:** These two outcomes are represented by the two basis vectors of the 2D Hilbert space:
        - $|\text{up}\rangle_z$ (or $|0\rangle$) = The state of "definite spin up."
        - $|\text{down}\rangle_z$ (or $|1\rangle$) = The state of "definite spin down." 
    - A general state $|\psi\rangle = \alpha |\text{up}\rangle_z + \beta |\text{down}\rangle_z$ is a _superposition_ of these two definite outcomes.
     
2. **Continuous Basis (e.g., a Particle in 1D):**
    - **The Measurement:** You decide to measure the particle's position.
    - **The Outcomes:** There is a continuum of possible, definite results: "found at position $x$."
    - **The Basis Vectors:** These infinite outcomes are represented by the infinite set of basis vectors $\{|x\rangle\}$.
    - The state $|x\rangle$ is a (non-physical, non-normalizable) "ideal" state where the particle is _perfectly_ located at the single point $x$.
    - A general state $|\psi\rangle = \int \psi(x) |x\rangle dx$ is a _continuous superposition_ of all possible position outcomes.
Of course. This is an excellent point to clarify. Here is that explanation in the note format, specifically addressing the meaning of the components of a _basis vector itself_.

## 5. The Basis Vector in Detail

The key concept is that a vector's components (the list of numbers like $a_{11}, a_{21}$) are **not** an intrinsic property of the vector. They are a _representation_ of that vector _relative to a chosen basis_.

This leads to two different physical interpretations for the components of a basis vector.

#### Case 1: A Basis Vector in its Own Basis (The "Trivial" Case)

This case defines the basis itself and is the mathematical statement of "definite, exclusive outcomes."

Let's say we have a 2-dimensional Hilbert space (like a qubit). We must _choose_ a basis. Let's choose the basis corresponding to a "spin-Z" measurement. This basis consists of two vectors:

- $|0\rangle$ (our first basis vector, representing "spin up")
- $|1\rangle$ (our second basis vector, representing "spin down")

Now, we represent these vectors as column vectors **in this very same $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ basis**.

By definition, the vector $|0\rangle$ is the state that has "1" part $|0\rangle$ and "0" parts $|1\rangle$.

$$|0\rangle \rightarrow \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} \\ a_{21} \end{pmatrix}$$

**Physical Interpretation:**

- **$a_{11} = 1$:** This is the probability amplitude $\langle 0 | 0 \rangle$. The probability of measuring the state as "spin up" _given that it is in the "spin up" state_ is $P = |a_{11}|^2 = |1|^2 = 1$ (100%).
- **$a_{21} = 0$:** This is the probability amplitude $\langle 1 | 0 \rangle$. The probability of measuring the state as "spin down" _given that it is in the "spin up" state_ is $P = |a_{21}|^2 = |0|^2 = 0$ (0%).

**Conclusion (Case 1):** In this context, the components are simply the mathematical definition of an **orthonormal basis**. They state that the basis vectors represent outcomes that are **definite** (100% chance of being itself) and **mutually exclusive** (0% chance of being another basis vector).

#### Case 2: A Basis Vector in a _Different_ Basis (The "Change of Basis" Case)

This is the more powerful physical interpretation. A vector that is a "definite outcome" for one measurement is a _superposition_ for a _different_ measurement.

Let's use the same 2D space, but now consider **two different bases**:

1. **Z-Basis (our "reference"):** $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ (for measuring spin up/down).
2. **X-Basis (a _different_ measurement):** $\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$ (for measuring spin right/left).

The vectors $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ are _also_ basis vectors. They are just as "fundamental" as $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$.

Now, let's take one of the X-basis vectors, say $|+\rangle$. What are its components in the Z-basis?

Physics tells us that the "spin right" state is an equal superposition of "spin up" and "spin down".
$$|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|0\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|1\rangle$$

So, the basis vector $|+\rangle$, when represented in the Z-basis $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$, is:
$$|+\rangle_Z \rightarrow \begin{pmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} \\ a_{21} \end{pmatrix}$$

**Physical Interpretation:**

- **$a_{11} = 1/\sqrt{2}$:** This is the **probability amplitude** $\langle 0 | + \rangle$. It's the amplitude that a system prepared in the definite state $|+\rangle$ ("spin right") will be measured as $|0\rangle$ ("spin up") if you perform a $Z$-measurement.
    - The probability is $P(\text{up}) = |a_{11}|^2 = |1/\sqrt{2}|^2 = 1/2$.

- **$a_{21} = 1/\sqrt{2}$:** This is the **probability amplitude** $\langle 1 | + \rangle$. It's the amplitude that the _same_ system in state $|+\rangle$ will be measured as $|1\rangle$ ("spin down").
    - The probability is $P(\text{down}) = |a_{21}|^2 = |1/\sqrt{2}|^2 = 1/2$.

**Conclusion (Case 2):**

The components of a basis vector from one basis (e.g., $X$-basis) written in another basis (e.g., $Z$-basis) are the probability amplitudes that connect the two different types of measurements. They are the "recipe" for building one "definite outcome" out of a superposition of the other "definite outcomes."

## 2. Key Properties of Basis Vectors

The mathematical properties of a basis directly map to physical requirements for measurement outcomes. For a valid set of measurement outcomes, they must be **mutually exclusive** and **complete**.

### a. Orthogonality (Mutually Exclusive)

Basis vectors are required to be **orthogonal** (perpendicular).

- **Mathematics:** The inner product of any two _different_ basis vectors is zero.
    - Discrete: $\langle \text{up} | \text{down} \rangle_z = 0$
    - Continuous: $\langle x' | x \rangle = 0$ (for $x' \neq x$)
- **Physical Interpretation:** This means the outcomes are mutually exclusive. If we perform a measurement and find the electron is "spin up," you are guaranteed that it is not "spin down." The probability of finding it in the "down" state when it is in the "up" state is zero: $$P(\text{up} \rightarrow \text{down}) = |\langle \text{down} | \text{up} \rangle|^2 = 0$$
### b. Normalization (Probabilities Sum to 1)

Basis vectors are typically **normalized** (have a length of 1).

- **Mathematics:** The inner product of a basis vector with itself is one.
    
    - Discrete: $\langle \text{up} | \text{up} \rangle_z = 1$
        
    - (Note: The continuous basis $|x\rangle$ is an exception. It's "normalized" to a Dirac delta function, $\langle x' | x \rangle = \delta(x-x')$, which is a mathematical tool for handling the infinite dimension.)
        
- Physical Interpretation: This sets the probability. It means if the system is already in the "spin up" state, the probability of measuring it to be "spin up" is $100\%$:
    
    $$P(\text{up} \rightarrow \text{up}) = |\langle \text{up} | \text{up} \rangle|^2 = 1^2 = 1$$
    

A set of vectors that is both **orthogonal** and **normalized** is called **orthonormal**.

### c. Completeness (All Outcomes Accounted For)

The set of basis vectors must **span the entire space**. This property is called **completeness**.

- **Mathematics:** This is expressed by the **closure relation**. The sum (or integral) of the "outer products" of all basis vectors must equal the identity operator $\mathbb{I}$.
    - Discrete: $|\text{up}\rangle\langle \text{up}| + |\text{down}\rangle\langle \text{down}| = \mathbb{I}$
    - Continuous: $\int |x\rangle\langle x| dx = \mathbb{I}$
- **Physical Interpretation:** This is the mathematical statement which says that the set of outcomes is complete, and we have accounted for all possibilities. There are no other results that the measurement can give. When we measure, the system must be found in one of the basis states.
    The completeness relation is an essential tool. We can insert it anywhere in an expression. For example, it's how we prove the normalization of a state $|\psi\rangle$: $$1 = \langle \psi | \psi \rangle = \langle \psi | \mathbb{I} | \psi \rangle = \langle \psi | \left( \sum_i |e_i\rangle\langle e_i| \right) | \psi \rangle$$$$1 = \sum_i \langle \psi | e_i \rangle \langle e_i | \psi \rangle = \sum_i c_i^* c_i = \sum_i |c_i|^2$$
    This shows that the completeness of the basis is what guarantees that the sum of all probabilities is 1.

# The Schwarz Inequality (or Cauchy-Schwarz)
The Schwarz inequality is a fundamental mathematical "speed limit" for an inner product. It's a theorem that relates the inner product of two vectors to their individual norms (lengths).

## 1. Conceptual View

The Schwarz inequality is the generalization of a simple geometric fact already known from 3D vectors.

- **In 3D:** The dot product is $\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = ||\vec{A}|| \cdot ||\vec{B}|| \cos\theta$.
- The _magnitude_ of this dot product is $|\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B}| = ||\vec{A}|| \cdot ||\vec{B}|| \cdot |\cos\theta|$.
- Since the value of $|\cos\theta|$ is always less than or equal to 1, we know that: $$|\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B}| \le ||\vec{A}|| \cdot ||\vec{B}||$$

**Intuition:** This just says that the "projection" of $\vec{A}$ onto $\vec{B}$ (which is what the dot product measures) can never be "longer" than $\vec{A}$'s full length.

The Schwarz inequality states that this _exact same rule_ holds for _all_ vector spaces, including our complex Hilbert spaces.

## 2. Formal Statement

For any two vectors $|\psi\rangle$ and $|\phi\rangle$ in a Hilbert space, the Schwarz inequality states:

$$|\langle \phi | \psi \rangle|^2 \le \langle \phi | \phi \rangle \langle \psi | \psi \rangle$$

- $|\langle \phi | \psi \rangle|^2$ is the **squared magnitude of the inner product**.
- $\langle \phi | \phi \rangle = ||\phi||^2$ is the **squared norm of $|\phi\rangle$**.
- $\langle \psi | \psi \rangle = ||\psi||^2$ is the **squared norm of $|\psi\rangle$**.

We can take the square root of both sides to get the equivalent form:
$$|\langle \phi | \psi \rangle| \le ||\phi|| \cdot ||\psi||$$

## 3. Physical Interpretation

This inequality is not just a mathematical curiosity; it is the **fundamental guarantee that our probabilistic interpretation of quantum mechanics is consistent.**

In quantum mechanics, all physical states $|\psi\rangle$ and $|\phi\rangle$ are **normalized**, which means their norms are 1.

- $\langle \phi | \phi \rangle = 1$
- $\langle \psi | \psi \rangle = 1$

Now, let's plug these values into the Schwarz inequality:

$$|\langle \phi | \psi \rangle|^2 \le 1 \cdot 1$$
$$|\langle \phi | \psi \rangle|^2 \le 1$$

We know from the Born rule that $P(\psi \rightarrow \phi) = |\langle \phi | \psi \rangle|^2$ is the probability of finding a system in state $|\psi\rangle$ to be in the state $|\phi\rangle$.

The Schwarz inequality is the _mathematical proof_ that this probability will **never be greater than 1**. It ensures our formalism never "breaks" by predicting a 120% probability, which would be physical nonsense.

### The "Equality" Case

The inequality becomes an equality, $|\langle \phi | \psi \rangle|^2 = \langle \phi | \phi \rangle \langle \psi | \psi \rangle$, if and only if the two vectors are **linearly dependent** (i.e., "parallel"), i.e.  $|\psi\rangle = \alpha |\phi\rangle$ (for some scalar $\alpha$).

**Physical Interpretation:**

This means the two kets $|\psi\rangle$ and $\alpha |\phi\rangle$ represent the exact same physical state. If the states are the same, the probability of finding one in the other should be 1. The equality condition confirms this: $P = |\langle \phi | \psi \rangle|^2 = 1$.

### A Major Application: The Uncertainty Principle

The Schwarz inequality is not just a "safety check." It's a powerful calculational tool. Its most famous application is in the **general derivation of the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle**. The proof of $\sigma_A^2 \sigma_B^2 \ge \left( \frac{1}{2i} \langle [\hat{A}, \hat{B}] \rangle \right)^2$ starts by applying the Schwarz inequality to two specially constructed state vectors.

## <u>Vector Space</u>

### 1. What is a Vector Space?

At its core, a **vector space** (or _linear space_) is a "playground" for vectors.

It's a collection of objects called **vectors** (like the kets $|\psi\rangle, |\phi\rangle, \dots$) and a set of **scalars** (like the complex numbers $\alpha, \beta, \dots$). This playground has a strict set of rules (axioms) for two fundamental operations:

1. **Vector Addition:** How to add two vectors (like $|\psi\rangle + |\phi\rangle$) to get a new vector.
2. **Scalar Multiplication:** How to multiply a vector by a scalar (like $\alpha |\psi\rangle$) to get a new vector. 

### 2. Formal Definition of a Vector Space

A vector space $V$ over a field of scalars $F$ is a set of vectors with two operations, **addition** and **scalar multiplication**, that must satisfy the following ten axioms.

1. Let $\psi$, $\phi$, and $\chi$ be any three vectors in the space $V$.
2. Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be any two scalars from the field $\mathbb{C}$.

#### A. Axioms for Vector Addition (Forms an Abelian Group)

1. **Closure:** If $\psi$ and $\phi$ are in $V$, then their sum $\psi + \phi$ is also in $V$.     
2. **Commutativity:** The order of addition doesn't matter.
$$\psi + \phi = \phi + \psi$$
3. **Associativity:** When adding three or more vectors, the grouping doesn't matter.
$$(\psi + \phi) + \chi = \psi + (\phi + \chi)$$

4. **Existence of a Zero Vector:** There exists a vector, the "null" or "zero" vector $0$, such that for any vector $\psi$:
$$\psi + 0 = \psi$$
5. **Existence of an Additive Inverse:** For every vector $\psi$, there exists an inverse vector, denoted $(-\psi)$, such that:
$$\psi + (-\psi) = 0$$

#### B. Axioms for Scalar Multiplication

6. **Closure:** If $\psi$ is in $V$ and $\alpha$ is a scalar in $\mathbb{C}$, then the product $\alpha \psi$ is also in $V$.
7. **Associativity:** The grouping of scalar multiplication doesn't matter.
$$\alpha(\beta \psi) = (\alpha\beta) \psi$$
8. **Distributivity over Vector Addition:** A scalar distributes to a sum of vectors.
$$\alpha (\psi + \phi) = \alpha \psi + \alpha \phi$$
9. **Distributivity over Scalar Addition:** A sum of scalars distributes to a vector.
$$(\alpha + \beta) \psi = \alpha \psi + \beta \psi$$
10. **Existence of a Multiplicative Identity:** There exists a scalar $1$ (the "identity element"), such that for any vector $\psi$::
$$1 \cdot \psi = \psi$$

Any set of objects that obeys all **ten** of these rules _is_ a *vector space*.

### 3. Terminologies in Vector Space

#### I. Field
A **field** is the set of **scalars** (the "numbers") that can be used to multiply our vectors. As per the axioms, these scalars can be added, subtracted, multiplied, and divided (by anything except zero).

- **Examples:** The set of **real numbers ($\mathbb{R}$)**, the set of **complex numbers ($\mathbb{C}$)**. 
- **For Quantum Mechanics:** The vector spaces used are defined over the field of **complex numbers ($\mathbb{C}$)**.

#### II. Linear Independence

This concept is used for building a set of "unique" axes for a vector space.

- **Definition:** A set of vectors $\{\phi_1, \phi_2, \dots, \phi_n\}$ is said to be linearly independent if the only solution to the equation $$\alpha_1 \phi_1 + \alpha_2 \phi_2 + \dots + \alpha_n \phi_n = 0$$...is the trivial one: $\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \dots = \alpha_n = 0$.
- If a non-trivial solution (where at least one $\alpha_i$ is not zero) exists, the set is **linearly dependent**.

**Interpretation:**

- **Independent:** "This set of vectors is efficient." No vector in the set can be built by adding and scaling the other vectors in the set. Each vector provides a "new direction" that is not reachable using the others.

- **Dependent:** "This set is redundant." At least one vector is a "freeloader"—it can be written as a combination of the others. For example, if $\phi_3 = \alpha_1 \phi_1 + \alpha_2 \phi_2$, then the set $\{\phi_1, \phi_2, \phi_3\}$ is linearly dependent.

#### III. Span

The span tells us "how much" of the vector space we can "reach" with a given set of vectors.

- **Definition:** The span of a set of vectors $\{\phi_1, \dots, \phi_n\}$ is the set of all possible vectors $\psi$ that can be created by forming linear combinations of them: $$\psi = \alpha_1 \phi_1 + \alpha_2 \phi_2 + \dots + \alpha_n \phi_n$$...for any set of scalars $\{\alpha_i\}$.

**Interpretation:**

If these set of vectors are considered as the "building blocks," their span is everything we can possibly build with them.
- The span of the single vector $z$ (in 3D) is the entire $z$ -axis.
- The span of the two vectors $\{x, y\}$ (in 3D) is the entire $x-y$ plane.
- If the span of a set of vectors is the _entire vector space_ $V$, we say the set is **complete**

#### IV. Basis

The basis combines the ideas of linear independence and spanning.

- **Definition:** A set of vectors $\{e_1, e_2, \dots, e_n\}$ is a **basis** for a vector space $V$ if it satisfies two conditions:

1. The set is **linearly independent**.
2. The set **spans the entire space,** $V$.

**Interpretation:**

A basis is a "perfect" set of coordinate axes for the vector space.
- **Spanning** means it has _enough_ vectors to build any vector $|\psi\rangle$ in the space.
- **Linearly independent** means it's not "wasteful"—it has the _minimum necessary_ number of vectors to do the job.

The power of a basis is that it allows any abstract vector $\psi$ to be represented uniquely as a set of scalar components $(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n)$ with respect to that basis.
$$\psi = \alpha_1 e_1 + \alpha_2 e_2 + \dots + \alpha_n e_n$$
This set of components $(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n)$ is the concrete "representation" of the abstract vector $\psi$ in the $\{e_i\}$ basis.

#### V. Dimension

The dimension defines the "size" of the vector space.

- **Definition:** The **dimension** of a vector space is the **number of vectors in any basis** for that space.
- A key theorem states that while a space can have many different bases, _every single basis_ for that space will contain the _same number_ of vectors. This number is an intrinsic, fundamental property of the space.

**Interpretations:**

- A vector space with 3 basis vectors is a **3-dimensional space**.
- The state space for a single **qubit** (like an electron's spin) is spanned by the basis $\{|\uparrow\rangle, |\downarrow\rangle\}$. It has two basis vectors, so it is a **2-dimensional** complex vector space.
- The state space for a particle's position in 1D is spanned by the _continuous_ set of vectors $\{|x\rangle\}$. This is an **infinite-dimensional** vector space.


## <u>Hilbert Space</u>
### 1. What is a Hilbert Space?

A **Hilbert Space** ($\mathcal{H}$) is the mathematical "arena" where quantum mechanics happens. It is a **complex vector space** that is also equipped with an **inner product**.

**Intuition:**
If a vector space is a "playground", the inner product is the "measuring tape" of that playground.

- It lets you measure the "length" of any vector.
- It lets you measure the "angle" between any two vectors.

In quantum mechanics, these "lengths" and "angles" have profound physical meaning: they correspond to **probabilities** and **orthogonality** (mutually exclusive outcomes).

The formal definition is:
> **A Hilbert space is a complete, complex inner product space.**

### 2. The Inner Product (or Scalar Product)

The inner product is the fundamental mathematical tool in Hilbert space that allows us to extract physical, measurable predictions from the abstract state vectors. It is an operation that takes _two_ vectors (say, $|\phi\rangle$ and $|\psi\rangle$) and combines them to produce a single **scalar** (a complex number).

We write this operation in Dirac's "bra-ket" notation, which is perfectly suited for this.

- $|\psi\rangle$ is the **ket** vector.
- $\langle \phi |$ is the **bra** vector.
- The inner product is given by $\langle \phi | \psi \rangle$.

#### Properties (Axioms) of the Inner Product

The inner product is _defined_ by the following rules, which must hold for any vectors $|\psi\rangle, |\phi\rangle, |\chi\rangle$ and any scalar $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$:

1. **Conjugate Symmetry:** Swapping the vectors conjugates the inner product. $$\langle \phi | \psi \rangle = \langle \psi | \phi \rangle^*$$
2. **Linearity in the Ket:** The inner product is linear in its second argument (the "ket"). $$\langle \chi | \left( \alpha|\psi\rangle + \beta|\phi\rangle \right) = \alpha \langle \chi | \psi \rangle + \beta \langle \chi | \phi \rangle$$
3. **Positive-Definiteness:** The inner product of a vector with itself is a positive, real number. The _only_ vector with zero length is the null vector: $\langle \psi | \psi \rangle = 0 \iff |\psi\rangle = |0\rangle$. $$\langle \psi | \psi \rangle \ge 0$$

### 3. Physical Interpretation of the Inner Product

- **The Norm (Length):** We define the norm of a state $|\psi\rangle$ as: $$||\psi|| = \sqrt{\langle \psi | \psi \rangle}$$ As we know, the probability of any outcome must be 1 (normalization). We enforce this by requiring that all physical state vectors have a "length" of 1: $$\langle \psi | \psi \rangle = 1$$ This is why we need the inner product. It's the tool that enforces the normalization of probability.

- **Orthogonality (Angles):** In 3D, if the dot product of two vectors is zero, they are perpendicular. The same is true here. Two vectors $|\psi\rangle$ and $|\phi\rangle$ are orthogonal if their inner product is zero: $$\langle \phi | \psi \rangle = 0$$
	- **Physical Interpretation:** It means the two states are **mutually exclusive**. If a system is in state $|\psi\rangle$, the probability of finding it in state $|\phi\rangle$ is zero: $P(\psi \rightarrow \phi) = |\langle \phi | \psi \rangle|^2 = 0$.
    - **Example:** The spin-up $|+\rangle$ and spin-down $|-\rangle$ states of an electron are orthogonal: $\langle + | - \rangle = 0$.

### 4. The "Completeness" Condition (The Fine Print)

This is a subtle mathematical property that is crucial for _infinite-dimensional_ spaces (like the space of wave functions $\psi(x)$).

- **Definition:** "Completeness" means the space is not "missing" any points. Any "well-behaved" infinite sequence of vectors in the space (a "Cauchy sequence") will converge to a limit _that is also a vector in that same space_.
- **Intuition:** The space has no "holes" in it. The set of rational numbers (fractions) is _not_ complete, because we can have a sequence of rational numbers (e.g., 3, 3.1, 3.14, 3.141, ...) that converges to $\pi$, which is _not_ a rational number. A Hilbert space doesn't have this problem.
- **Physical Interpretation:** This condition ensures that our Hilbert space can handle infinite superpositions and limits (like Fourier series) without "breaking." It guarantees that any state we can construct physically has a valid vector in our space. For finite-dimensional spaces (like a qubit), the space is automatically complete.

### 5. Cauchy Sequence

In simple terms, a **Cauchy sequence** is a sequence of vectors where the vectors get _arbitrarily close to each other_ as you go further along in the sequence.

Let's imagine a sequence of vectors: $|\psi_1\rangle, |\psi_2\rangle, |\psi_3\rangle, \dots$ This sequence is "Cauchy" if the "distance" (norm) between any two vectors far out in the sequence, say $|\psi_n\rangle$ and $|\psi_m\rangle$ (where $n$ and $m$ are very large), is almost zero.

- **Formal Idea:** For any tiny distance $\epsilon > 0$ we choose, there is a point $N$ in the sequence such that _all_ vectors after $N$ are within $\epsilon$ of _each other_.
- **Intuition:** The sequence "bunches up" on itself. It's clear the sequence is _trying_ to settle down and converge to _something_.

#### The "Hole" Problem (Cauchy vs. Convergent)

This is the key. We have to ask: "Okay, the sequence is bunching up, but is the _thing_ it's bunching up _around_ actually _in our space_?"

- A **Convergent Sequence** is a sequence that gets arbitrarily close to a _specific limit vector_ $|\psi\rangle$ that _is in the space_. (Every convergent sequence is automatically a Cauchy sequence).
- A **Cauchy Sequence** is just one that "bunches up," but we don't know (yet) if its limit is in the space.

#### The Best Analogy: Rational Numbers vs. Real Numbers

This is the classic example that makes it all clear.

- **The Space:** Let's say our "vector space" is only the set of **rational numbers** (fractions, $\mathbb{Q}$).
- **The Sequence:** Consider the sequence of numbers for $\pi$:
    - $x_1 = 3$
    - $x_2 = 3.1$
    - $x_3 = 3.14$
    - $x_4 = 3.141$
    - $x_5 = 3.1415$
    - ... (all of these are rational numbers)
- **Is it Cauchy?** **Yes.** The terms are getting closer and closer _to each other_. The distance between $x_{1000}$ and $x_{1001}$ is tiny. The sequence is "bunching up."
- **Is it Convergent?** **No.** It _is_ converging, but its limit is $\pi$. And $\pi$ is _not_ a rational number. It's not in our space $\mathbb{Q}$.

This is an **incomplete space**. It has "holes" in it. It contains a Cauchy sequence ($x_n$) whose limit ($\pi$) is _not_ in the space.

#### What "Complete" Means

A space is **complete** if it has **no holes**.

> **A space is "complete" if every Cauchy sequence in that space converges to a limit that is _also_ in that space.**

The set of **real numbers ($\mathbb{R}$)** _is_ complete. The sequence (3, 3.1, 3.14, ...) is a Cauchy sequence, and its limit $\pi$ _is_ a real number. So it's both Cauchy and convergent.

#### Physical Meaning in Hilbert Space

A **Hilbert space** is _defined_ as being **complete**. This is a non-negotiable axiom.

- **Why?** Because in quantum mechanics, we constantly use _infinite superpositions_ (infinite sums).
- **Example:** A general state in an infinite-dimensional basis (like the energy states of a harmonic oscillator) is: $$|\psi\rangle = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n |n\rangle$$
- This infinite sum is _defined_ as the **limit** of a sequence of partial sums:
    - $|\psi_1\rangle = c_1 |1\rangle$
    - $|\psi_2\rangle = c_1 |1\rangle + c_2 |2\rangle$
    - $|\psi_3\rangle = c_1 |1\rangle + c_2 |2\rangle + c_3 |3\rangle$
    - ...
- This sequence is a **Cauchy sequence** (as long as $\sum |c_n|^2$ converges, which is the normalization condition).
- The **completeness** of the Hilbert space is the mathematical _guarantee_ that the limit vector $|\psi\rangle$ is a valid, well-defined vector that is _in the space_.

**In short: "Completeness" ensures that our Hilbert space can handle infinite sums (like Fourier series or infinite superpositions) without "breaking." Any state that we can build as a limit is guaranteed to have a "home" in our space.** This is critical for wave mechanics.

## <u>Square Integrable Functions</u>

### 1. What are Square-Integrable Functions?

**Square-integrable functions are the set of all "physically allowed" wave functions.**

When we solve the Schrödinger equation, we often find many mathematical solutions (like $e^x$ or $x^2$), but most of them are not physically possible. The "square-integrable" condition is the mathematical "filter" we use to separate the physically realistic solutions from the non-physical ones.

**Intuition:**

A physical particle must be somewhere. The probability of finding it somewhere in the universe must be 100%. A function that is "square-integrable" is a function that is "well-behaved" enough that we can normalize it to represent this 100% probability.

### 2. The Formal Definition

A function $\psi(x)$ is called **square-integrable** if the integral of its magnitude-squared, over all space, is a **finite number**.

For a one-dimensional function:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\psi(x)|^2 \, dx < \infty$$

- This integral must not "blow up" to infinity. It must converge to some finite value.

### 3. The Physical Interpretation (The "Why")

This definition is not arbitrary. It comes directly from the **Born Rule**.

1. **Probability Density:** The Born rule states that the quantity $|\psi(x)|^2$ is the **probability density**—the probability _per unit length_ of finding the particle at position $x$.

2. **Total Probability:** To find the total probability of finding the particle anywhere at all, we must integrate this probability density over all possible positions (from $x = -\infty$ to $x = +\infty$). $$P_{\text{total}} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\psi(x)|^2 \, dx$$
3. **The "Entrance Ticket":** For a state to be physical, this total probability must be 100%, or $P_{\text{total}} = 1$. This is the **normalization condition**.

**The key connection is:**

A function must be square-integrable (integral is finite) so that it can be normalized (scaled by a constant to make the integral exactly 1).

- If a function is _not_ square-integrable (e.g., $\psi(x) = C$, a constant for all $x$), its integral $\int |C|^2 dx$ is infinite. There is no normalization constant that can make an infinite value equal to 1. This function cannot represent a physical particle because the total probability of finding it is infinite, which is meaningless.

- If a function is square-integrable (e.g., a Gaussian $\psi(x) = e^{-ax^2}$), its integral is a finite number (say, $N$). We can then create a valid, normalized wave function: $\psi_{\text{norm}}(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} e^{-ax^2}$. This new function is now properly normalized: $$\int |\psi_{\text{norm}}(x)|^2 dx = \int \frac{1}{N} |e^{-ax^2}|^2 dx = \frac{1}{N} \int |e^{-ax^2}|^2 dx = \frac{1}{N} \cdot N = 1$$

So, being "square-integrable" is the "entrance ticket" for a function to be considered a physical state.

### 4. The $L^2$ Hilbert Space

The set of _all_ square-integrable functions is a vector space. This space is called **$L^2(\mathbb{R})$** (for 1D) or $L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$ (for 3D).

This $L^2$ space _is_ the Hilbert space that fulfills all the requirements:

1. **It is a Vector Space:**
    - We can add two square-integrable functions, and their sum is still square-integrable.
    - We can multiply a square-integrable function by a scalar, and it's still square-integrable.
2. **It has an Inner Product:**
    We define the inner product for this function space in a way that matches the abstract axioms. For any two functions $\phi(x)$ and $\psi(x)$ in $L^2$, the inner product $\langle \phi | \psi \rangle$ is defined as the integral: $$\langle \phi | \psi \rangle \equiv \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi^*(x) \psi(x) \, dx$$The norm-squared $\langle \psi | \psi \rangle$ is just $\int \psi^*(x) \psi(x) dx = \int |\psi(x)|^2 dx$. This confirms that our inner product correctly gives the total probability.
3. **It is Complete:**
    This is a non-trivial mathematical theorem (the **Riesz-Fischer Theorem**). It states that the $L^2$ space has no "holes." Any Cauchy sequence of square-integrable functions converges to a limit that is also a square-integrable function.
    - **Physical Meaning:** This is what guarantees that we can build complex states out of infinite superpositions (like Fourier series) and the resulting state will still be a valid, physical wave function in our Hilbert space.
